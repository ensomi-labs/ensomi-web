Why ensomi

Most of the time, music is something we listen to. A rhythm game lets you step inside a song: your hands follow the beat, and listening turns into playing. But a song becomes playable only after someone charts it by hand, note by note, and most of the music in your life never gets a chart.

We are changing that. ensomi learns from charts that mappers have made and writes new ones from the audio, so that the music you love, and the music already around you, can become something you play.

And that is only the start. What becomes possible when a model truly understands music, and everyone can build on it? New ways to play, to create, to work with sound. We are exploring that in the open, with the community that made all of this possible.

Our mission

Turn music understanding and rhythm-game creation into open tools for players, mappers and developers, until listening to music, playing it and creating with it become one activity. These are the four frontiers we are exploring.

Ambient play

The music around you becomes something you play.

A song is already playing nearby. ensomi recognises it, finds where it is, and starts a chart, generated in real time, that follows the music without interrupting it.

Open music understanding

Your ear leads; the timing follows.

A mapper drops in a song and ensomi proposes its beats and tempo, even where the meter shifts or the pulse is hard to read. Where their ear disagrees, they mark what they hear, and the timing is worked out again around that mark. Open by design, so DAWs, games and tools nobody has imagined yet can build on the same understanding of music.

Training

Practise on music you love.

A player picks songs they love and a movement they want to get better at, and the chart is shaped around that movement.

Playing together

Your best play lands in your opponent's chart.

Like clearing lines in versus Tetris, a clean passage or a long combo sends an attack: a burst of notes or a harder pattern, dropped into what your opponent is about to play. In co-op you coordinate instead, and what one of you plays shapes the other's chart.

R2 is our first step: a whole song, charted while you wait, and you can already ask for a style in one part of it. From here, we are going after charts that play along in real time, change as you play and respond to what you hear.

R2: from a song to a chart you can play

R2 is our first publicly playable release. It works from the audio alone, and between your song and your chart, three things happen.

  1. It finds the beat.R2 listens for the beats, then lines each tempo section up with where the song's sounds begin.
  2. It chooses where notes fall.Beat by beat, it decides where notes start, from what it hears.
  3. It arranges the chart.It takes a character, such as how many long notes and chords, from human charts whose notes fall in a similar way, then lays out lanes, taps and long notes. This step does not hear the audio yet.

What comes out is a complete osu!mania 4K chart, made in your browser, that you can watch against the music and open in osu!.

R2 and the rows model were trained on osu!'s ranked and loved 4K charts from 2 to 6 stars. We do not distribute those charts or their audio. See data provenance for how they were used and for corrections.

Next: N1, R3

Two models, one direction. N1 learns to understand music. R3 turns that understanding into charts that feel written for the song.

N1 · open music understanding

A model that hears how music is built.

N1 starts with timing: the beats, tempo changes and offsets every mapper works out before placing a single note. We are building it as open infrastructure for rhythm-game charting and open DAW tools, under the MIT licence, code included.

  1. From song to tempo map

    Drop in a song and get a tempo map to work from. The first mapper tool is as direct as it sounds: audio in, an .osu with its red lines in place out.

  2. Mark what you hear

    Fix one beat, not every line. Mark what you hear and N1 reworks the timing around your mark, leaving the rest alone.

  3. Beyond the beat grid

    A grid is only the surface of a song. We are teaching a model to work out how music is put together: the pulse, the attacks, odd meters and the layers a listener moves between.

  4. The hardest music first

    Tempo changes, free tempo, beats you can read more than one way. We are aiming N1 at the music that defeats today's tools, and we measure it by the work it saves people.

R3 · the next chart system

Charts that listen, all the way through.

Like R2, R3 spans timing, head rows (where notes fall) and choreography (how they are arranged). It will take its timing from N1, generate in real time, and go after what R2 taught us, in this order.

  1. Notes on the music

    Notes should land on what you hear. We are working on head rows that sit more precisely on what the audio actually contains.

  2. An arrangement that listens

    R2 lays out notes from where they fall, not from the sound. For R3 we are bringing music information into the arrangement model, so that long notes follow the music and the choreography listens too.

  3. Less hidden steering

    A few calibration rules hold R2 steady as it writes, such as a restoring force that pulls the chart back toward the character it was given. We are finding where they bend charts out of shape, so that the music can lead.

  4. Difficulty you can trust

    When you ask for a star rating, the chart should match it. We are working on difficulty control that delivers exactly that, even if it means reshaping much of the system.

Follow the research, and help shape it, on GitHub.

Run it yourself

The generator above runs R2 in your browser with @ensomi/runtime: the networks in ONNX Runtime Web, everything else in ensomi's Rust runtime compiled to WebAssembly. The browser decodes the song and draws its own random numbers, so a seed here does not give the same chart as the same seed on the command line.

To run the release from the command line (we checked it on macOS with Apple silicon; Linux and CUDA were not run):

git clone https://github.com/ensomi-labs/ensomi-model.git
cd ensomi-model
git checkout --detach r2-1.0.1
uv sync --python 3.10 --extra mps
hf download sed-i/ensomi-r2 --revision r2-1.0.1 --local-dir artifacts/hf-r2

uv run --python 3.10 --extra mps python -m ensomi_model.system.make \
  --model-dir artifacts/hf-r2 --audio /path/to/song.mp3 --star 3.5 \
  --title "Song title" --artist "Artist" --seed 0 --out artifacts/my-chart.osz

The first run also downloads BeatThis's checkpoint (81 MB) from its authors. Decoding audio other than WAV needs ffmpeg. --help lists every request. Full instructions are on the Hugging Face card.
